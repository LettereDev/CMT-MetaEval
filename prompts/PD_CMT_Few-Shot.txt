According to the following description of how Conceptual Metaphor Theory (CMT) identifies and classifies Metaphors: Lakoff's (1993) theory identifies metaphor as a conceptual phenomenon rather than a purely linguistic one, arguing that metaphorical expression is conceptual and underlies both literal and poetic language. Metaphors are identified through cross-domain mappings in which a source domain structures a target domain, as in the example TIME IS MONEY. This mapping process distinguishes metaphor from metonymy, which operates within a single experiential domain rather than across two 
Lakoff and Johnson classify metaphors into three primary types: structural (one concept structured in terms of another, e.g., ARGUMENT IS WAR), orientational (spatial organization such as UP IS GOOD), and ontological (treating abstractions as entities or containers). Additional categories include synesthetic, simple, and extended metaphors . Basic-level conceptual metaphors are grounded in embodied human experience, making them likely to appear across languages and cultures.
